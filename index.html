Flat cash pays the same number of pounds every year. Over decades inflation quietly shrinks what that buys — £10,000 a year from age 20 is worth about £3,300 in today's money by 65, so the later payments do far less work than the early ones.

Rise with inflation uprates the payment each year by the inflation rate above, holding its buying power steady. This is usually the more realistic model, since contributions tend to track earnings. It raises the contributed figure a lot, because you are genuinely paying in more pounds — the fair comparison between the two modes is the today's-money number, not the nominal one.

Contributions go in at the start of each year, so every payment earns a full year of return, and the rate is applied as a constant annual figure. Real markets never deliver the same return twice, and the order of good and bad years changes where you land — treat this as the shape of compounding, not a forecast. Not financial advice.
